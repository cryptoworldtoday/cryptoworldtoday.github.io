US wins authority to seize $1.2bn from Binance hack

The Justice Department has court authority to seize or restrain $1.2 billion of Binance hack proceeds, within a wider effort that has frozen $2.7 billion.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 13, 2026 · 22:21 UTC3 min read

The U.S. Justice Department has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, moving a portion of the assets frozen since last month’s breach into a legal recovery process. The department said on Nov. 12 that government and private-sector efforts had frozen more than $2.7 billion in total; the $1.2 billion is part of that amount, not an additional recovery, according to its announcement of the court-authorized action.

The theft took place on or about Oct. 29. The department says North Korean military members, whom the FBI calls TraderTraitor, stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from the exchange. The FBI had attributed the attack to North Korea in an Oct. 31 alert. The Justice Department’s Nov. 12 announcement puts the court-authorized amount at more than $1.2 billion, while the wider freeze effort has reached more than $2.7 billion. Those figures describe different stages of control over the stolen assets.

That distinction matters. A freeze can prevent assets from moving while authorities investigate, but it does not by itself transfer ownership to the government or determine who should ultimately receive the funds. The Justice Department says it has obtained authority to “seize or restrain” the $1.2 billion; it did not say that forfeiture is complete or that the assets have been returned to Binance or its customers. The Associated Press, in its report on the seizure and recovery process, notes that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then decides who has a legitimate claim to recovered property.

The gap between the scale of the theft and the restrained funds remains substantial: the department’s figures put more than $2.7 billion under freeze against a theft exceeding $6 billion. The figures do not establish how much of the unfrozen balance remains recoverable, nor do they specify which tokens or addresses make up the court-authorized amount. They show that tracing and intervention have produced a significant result, while leaving ownership and final recovery unresolved.

The mechanism depends partly on cooperation from virtual asset service providers, or VASPs, which the department says worked with law enforcement to interdict funds. That approach can block assets when they reach services able to act on law-enforcement requests. It also leaves a harder tracing problem when funds move through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. The Justice Department says TraderTraitor actors continue to launder assets through those routes, and that efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit them are ongoing. The FBI, working with the U.S. Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation, has urged VASPs to block transactions involving addresses linked to the laundering.

The next measures to watch are whether the restrained funds become subject to forfeiture claims, how much of the remaining stolen balance can be frozen, and whether investigators can keep identifying assets as they move across services and networks. The $1.2 billion court authorization marks a move from freezing funds toward potential forfeiture; it does not settle who will receive them or how much of the original theft will ultimately be recovered.
