Why Wallets Switch Networks Before a Bridge Signature

A wallet switches to a bridge’s required chain to prepare the right signing context; the network prompt alone does not approve or move funds.

By The Coin Wire EditorialOct 17, 2026 · 00:53 UTC5 min read

A wallet switches networks before a bridge signature because the bridge needs the wallet connected to a particular chain for the next step. That step might be approving a token allowance, sending a deposit transaction, or signing a message; the switch itself does none of those things. It changes which network the wallet uses for chain-specific requests, so the prompt is a change of context, not proof that a transfer has started.

In a common route, the bridge first needs an action on the chain where the user’s tokens are held, then processes a corresponding action on the destination chain. Those actions may be separated by a wait while the bridge or its contracts verify the source transaction. Some routes use a token approval before the deposit, while others use a signature-based approval or combine steps. A closer comparison of super bridge routing and cross-chain swaps helps explain why routes can ask for different steps. The sequence depends on the bridge design and asset, not just on the wallet.

What does a network switch change?

A network switch changes the active chain used by the wallet’s connection to the app. On Ethereum-compatible wallets, an app can request this change with `wallet_switchEthereumChain`, passing the chain ID it needs. The wallet may accept or reject the request, and can ask the user to confirm it. After switching, requests for the current chain, such as reading a balance or preparing a transaction, are directed to that network.

The chain matters because transactions are prepared for a specific network. The wallet needs the right chain context to show and sign a transaction that can be submitted there. A bridge may therefore request a switch before it can present its source-chain transaction, or later when an action needs to be signed on the destination chain. A switch does not change the token’s location, bridge the funds, or grant an app permission to spend them.

That distinction is useful when a prompt appears unexpectedly. A switch prompt names a network; a token approval or transaction prompt describes an action and should show details such as the asset, contract or destination, and requested amount. A message-signing prompt is different again: it asks for a cryptographic signature, which may authorize a particular operation without submitting a conventional transaction at that moment.

Why does the switch happen before signing?

The app often requests the switch first because it must know which chain the wallet will use before it can prepare the next request. The wallet’s active chain and the chain required by the bridge may differ. If they do, the app can request a change, wait for the wallet to report the new chain, then construct the appropriate transaction or signature request.

For a token that needs a separate allowance, the flow may include a switch, an approval transaction, and then a deposit transaction. Each transaction is a separate user decision and may require a separate signature. If the token or route supports a permit-style signature, the app may ask for a message signature instead of a standalone approval transaction. A native asset transfer may also avoid the token-approval step. These variations explain why two bridges moving the same asset can show different prompt sequences.

The nearest alternative is to switch networks manually before opening or using the bridge. That can reduce surprise, but it does not remove the need for chain-specific approval or deposit requests, and the app may still ask to switch if the route changes. Wallets that can read from multiple chains may show balances across networks without changing the active chain, but signing still needs a clear chain context. An app’s multi-chain display does not mean the wallet can submit a transaction to every chain at once.

How can you tell what you are being asked to sign?

Check the network and the action separately. A switch prompt should identify the chain the app wants to use; a subsequent approval or transaction prompt should identify what will be authorized or sent. If the displayed chain does not match the step you expected, stop and confirm the route inside the bridge interface before approving anything. The main risk is not the switch by itself, but treating it as if it were the transaction or signing a request without understanding its scope.

  • Confirm the requested network matches the source or destination step shown by the bridge.
  • For an approval, check which token and spender are named, and whether the requested allowance matches your intent.
  • For a transaction, review the destination, asset, and amount before confirming.
  • If you reject a switch or signature, check the app’s status before retrying; the route may need to be restarted or refreshed.

The practical rule is to treat each prompt as a separate action: switching selects the chain context, a signature authorizes a message or transaction, and a submitted source transaction begins the on-chain part of the route. Watch which chain each prompt names, whether the bridge asks for an approval before the deposit, and how it reports completion on the destination side. Those signals reveal whether the flow is behaving as designed far better than the number of prompts alone.
