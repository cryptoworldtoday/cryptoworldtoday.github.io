Two explorers show what a bridge quote leaves out

A bridge quote is an estimate; two chain explorers show the source fee actually paid on-chain and whether a separate destination transaction completed.

By The Coin Wire EditorialOct 16, 2026 · 09:22 UTC5 min read

To check a crypto bridge’s cost, compare its quote with the source transaction fee in one chain explorer and confirm the destination transaction in the other. A quote describes the route before it runs; an explorer records what a particular transaction did on-chain. Those views answer different questions, and neither alone gives the whole cost.

A bridge may move the same token between chains, swap it for another token, or combine both steps. Its quoted total can include the source network fee, a bridge or routing charge, and an estimate of destination execution or token conversion. Some routes also need a separate token approval before the transfer, which can mean a second source-chain transaction and fee. For a closer look at route choices, see QuickSwap routes for treasury transfers. A route that looks cheaper at first may deliver a different token or amount, so compare the expected destination asset as well as the fee.

What does the source-chain explorer tell me?

The source-chain explorer shows whether the transaction was included, whether it succeeded, and how much network gas it actually used. Search the transaction hash from the wallet or bridge interface, then check the status, fee, sender, and token transfers. On Ethereum, for example, transaction fees depend on gas used and the price paid per gas unit; other chains display their own fee units and native tokens.

Compare the explorer’s actual fee with the quote’s source-network estimate, converting both into the same currency if the interface displays one in dollars and the other in the chain’s native token. The estimate can differ because network conditions changed between the quote and confirmation, or because the transaction used a different amount of gas than predicted. A failed transaction can still consume gas: failure means the requested operation did not complete, not necessarily that the network charged nothing.

Also check whether an approval preceded the bridge transaction. An approval lets a contract spend a token from the wallet; it is separate from the transfer itself and may have its own transaction hash and fee. If the bridge interface asked for approval, include that fee when calculating what it cost to start the transfer. For a route you have not submitted yet, a quote cannot show this final amount; it can only estimate it.

Why check the destination-chain explorer too?

The destination-chain explorer shows whether the expected asset arrived and whether a destination-side transaction was needed. A successful source transaction means the source chain accepted its part of the process. It does not prove that the cross-chain transfer has finished: the bridge may still be relaying a message, waiting for confirmations, or requiring a claim.

Use the destination network’s explorer to search the destination transaction hash if the bridge provides one. Confirm that the receiving address is yours, the token is the one you expected, and the amount matches the quote after any stated conversion or fee. If no destination hash appears, use the bridge’s own status page or transaction lookup with the source hash; a standard chain explorer generally cannot show every off-chain step in a bridge’s route.

The destination record also helps explain who paid for execution there. Some routes deduct destination costs from the transferred amount, some include them in the upfront quote, and some require a separate claim transaction from the recipient. In the last case, that claim can incur another network fee. The bridge’s fee breakdown and status labels matter here: “in transit” and “completed” describe different stages, and a completed source transaction alone should not be treated as proof of delivery.

How do I compare bridge routes fairly?

Compare routes by the amount and asset expected at the destination, the total estimated cost, and any extra transaction the route requires. Two quotes for the same nominal transfer can differ because one route swaps tokens, uses different liquidity, or handles destination execution differently. A lower quoted fee is not automatically better if the route delivers less value after conversion or requires another paid step.

  • Before signing, note the source and destination chains, token names, receiving address, quoted output, and itemized fees.
  • After the source transaction, record its actual fee and check its status in that chain’s explorer.
  • Follow the bridge status or destination hash, then verify receipt and any claim fee in the destination explorer.
  • Add any approval or claim transactions to the total; compare that sum with the original quote and other routes.

This check is more informative than comparing a bridge’s headline fee with a basic token transfer. A bridge transaction calls contracts and may perform several steps, while a simple transfer usually does less work; network fees also vary by chain and time. A centralized exchange withdrawal is another alternative, but its fee schedule and supported networks work differently, so it is not a like-for-like bridge quote. QuickSwap’s cross-chain routes are one example of a routed swap, where the destination asset and conversion are part of the comparison.

For most beginners, the useful number is the net amount expected to arrive after all required steps, alongside the total fees paid across both chains. Watch for a gap between quoted and actual source gas, a destination status that remains pending, a missing or unexpected token transfer, and any claim still required. Those signals show whether the apparent saving survived the trip across chains.
