Borderless’s 84% remittance saving meets a route-by-route test

Borderless puts a $200 stablecoin-funded remittance at $2, against $12.72 for traditional transfers, but the comparison spans different periods and costs vary by route.

By The Coin Wire EditorialOct 19, 2026 · 03:05 UTC3 min read

Stablecoin payments network Borderless.xyz says a $200 remittance sent over stablecoin-funded rails cost a median $2 in the third quarter of 2026, compared with $12.72 for a traditional transfer benchmark. The roughly 84% gap, reported by Crypto.news on Borderless’s Q3 figures, gives a fresh measure of the cost claim around stablecoin transfers. But the comparison pairs Borderless’s 2026 pricing with the World Bank’s 6.36% global average from Q3 2025; it does not compare the same routes during the same period.

On a $200 payment, the quoted rates mean $2 in stablecoin-funded costs against $12.72 for the traditional benchmark, a difference of $10.72. The percentages also describe different measures: Borderless reports a median, while the World Bank figure is a global average. That distinction, alongside the one-year gap, limits how precisely the 84% headline describes what a particular sender could save today.

How much of the saving survives against digital services?

The gap narrows when the comparison moves from the overall remittance market to digital alternatives. The World Bank’s Q3 2025 figures put digital remittances at an average 4.59% and digital-only money-transfer operators at 3.54%, below the overall 6.36% benchmark. Borderless’s reported 1% median is still lower, but those figures remain historical averages and do not establish the price on a particular corridor.

The comparison is relevant because the conventional alternative is not a single payment method. A sender may choose a bank, a money-transfer operator or a digital service, with different fees and exchange rates. The headline saving is largest against the overall benchmark; it is smaller against those digital measures. A route-matched, same-period comparison would make the relative advantage easier to judge.

What happens between the stablecoin transfer and local cash?

Moving tokens on a blockchain is only one part of a remittance. In its 30 July study, Banca d’Italia’s mystery-shopping exercise followed 200 USDC transfers across ten corridors linking Italy with Argentina, Brazil, South Africa, the United Arab Emirates and Japan. It found total costs ranging from 0.30% to nearly 9%, with no systematic cost advantage over traditional channels.

The study points to the entry and exit points as a key constraint: users need to move local currency into an exchange, buy stablecoins, then convert the tokens and withdraw local currency at the destination. The on-chain transfer accounted for only a marginal share of total cost in the exercise. Where instant payment systems were available, end-to-end settlement took under 20 minutes; where standard bank transfers were needed, it took one or two business days. Stablecoin settlement can be fast, but the full transfer still depends on local payment infrastructure.

The two findings measure different things. Borderless’s reported median and the World Bank’s global average offer a broad cost comparison, while Banca d’Italia tested selected routes using actual transfers. The latter found costs varied sharply by corridor, so its results do not erase Borderless’s reported gap; they show why a single average cannot predict every sender’s outcome.

Which numbers will show whether the gap holds?

The next useful comparison will put stablecoin and conventional prices on the same corridors and in the same period, with exchange-rate spreads and cash-out costs visible. Route-level completion and timing matter too: a low quoted fee is less useful if payout takes longer or fails to reach the recipient. For now, Borderless’s figures suggest a lower median cost against the broad traditional benchmark, while Banca d’Italia’s test shows that access to local payment rails can change the total price and speed substantially.
