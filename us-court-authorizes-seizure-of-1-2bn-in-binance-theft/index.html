US court authorizes seizure of $1.2bn in Binance theft

The Justice Department says court authorization covers more than $1.2 billion of Binance theft proceeds, while government and industry efforts have frozen over $2.7 billion.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 16, 2026 · 10:11 UTC3 min read

The Justice Department says it has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step within a broader effort that has frozen more than $2.7 billion. Its Nov. 12 announcement describes the $1.2 billion as part of the larger frozen total, not as a completed return of funds to Binance or its customers.

The distinction sets the scale of what has changed. The FBI attributes the theft of more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about Oct. 29 to North Korean military members it calls TraderTraitor. The government says it and private-sector partners have frozen more than $2.7 billion since the theft, and obtained court authorization over more than $1.2 billion of that amount. The Associated Press reported on the seizure announcement and noted that a judge would typically decide who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds through a forfeiture process.

So the headline figure measures a legal step, while the larger figure measures funds frozen through joint efforts. A freeze can restrict movement; court authorization to seize or restrain gives the government a further route to keep assets under control. The Justice Department says work to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the assets is ongoing. It does not say that all of the $1.2 billion has been transferred to government wallets, forfeited, or returned to victims.

The mechanism depends on cooperation across the crypto market. The department says US agencies worked with virtual asset service providers to interdict the stolen assets. The FBI had also urged providers to block transactions with, or derived from, addresses TraderTraitor uses to launder the funds. Investigators say the actors continue to move assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. That leaves a continuing contest between tracing and freezing flows across services and the attackers’ attempts to route them onward.

The $2.7 billion frozen so far is less than half of the more than $6 billion reported stolen. The gap does not establish how much remains recoverable: the department says efforts continue, and the assets are moving through multiple channels. But it does show why the authorized seizure matters alongside, rather than instead of, the wider freeze effort. Providers can help interrupt transfers quickly; courts and forfeiture proceedings determine what the government may ultimately take and who may claim it.

The case is also distinct from the separate FaceStealer actions announced the same day. The department says that North Korean social-engineering scheme stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over the past 18 months and involved cloud infrastructure that US authorities seized. Those actions concern another alleged operation, not the Binance theft or the $1.2 billion authorization.

The next signals are the amount ultimately seized and forfeited, any process for returning assets to legitimate claimants, and whether investigators can keep tracing funds as they move through bridges, mixers and exchanges. For now, the confirmed change is that a court-authorized action covers more than $1.2 billion of a theft exceeding $6 billion, against a combined government and industry freeze of more than $2.7 billion.
