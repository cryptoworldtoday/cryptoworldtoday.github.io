Wrong-network crypto: recovery depends on who controls the key

Sent crypto to the wrong network? Often you can get it back if you control the receiving wallet; exchange deposits need support, and contract cases may be unrecoverable.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 05:37 UTC5 min read

A wrong-network crypto transfer may still be recoverable, and the first step is to find out who controls the receiving address. A confirmed transaction stays on the chain where it was sent, but that does not always mean the funds are lost. Your options depend on whether the address belongs to your own wallet, an exchange, or a contract.

Sent crypto to the wrong network: can I get it back?

Often, yes, when the receiving address belongs to a wallet whose key you control. Start by finding the transaction hash in the explorer for the chain the transfer actually used. The hash shows whether the transfer succeeded, which address received the tokens, and which token contract was involved. Use the explorer for the actual chain, such as Arbitrum or Polygon, rather than the chain you meant to use.

Then identify the receiving address. If it is your own EVM wallet address, you may be able to access the funds by adding the network to your wallet. If it belongs to an exchange, the exchange holds the keys and must decide whether it can recover the deposit. If it is a contract address, recovery depends on the contract’s functions and who can operate them.

How do I recover crypto sent to my own wallet on the wrong network?

For your own EVM wallet, add the network where the transfer arrived and view the same address there. An EVM address is derived from a key pair, so the same externally owned account (EOA) address is controlled by that key across EVM chains. Ethereum.org explains how an account address comes from its public key in its Ethereum accounts documentation.

A token may not appear automatically. Check the explorer for the token contract address, then add the token to your wallet’s view on the receiving network. Confirm the chain and contract address carefully; a token with the same name on another chain may be a different asset.

Before moving tokens, make sure the wallet has that chain’s native asset to pay gas. ETH on Ethereum, for instance, does not pay transaction fees on every other chain. Once you can see the token and have gas, you can send it to your own address on the intended chain if the token exists there, or bridge it across. A cross-chain bridge aggregator such as lifi bridge compares routes for moving assets between networks; the linked topic explains how that route selection works. A bridge adds another transaction and its own fee, so compare the route details with the value you are recovering before you proceed.

What if I sent crypto to an exchange address on an unsupported network?

Contact the exchange’s support team with the transaction hash, asset, sending network, receiving address, and approximate time. Do not try to bridge funds from an exchange deposit address: the exchange controls that address’s keys, not your wallet. It may need to locate the deposit and decide whether it can return it to you.

Recovery follows the exchange’s policy. Some exchanges may be unable or unwilling to recover assets sent through unsupported networks; others may offer recovery for a fee or after a delay. Ask support for the available process and any charge before sending further funds. Never share your seed phrase or private key with someone claiming they can recover the deposit.

Can a confirmed transaction be cancelled or reversed?

No: once a transaction is confirmed, you cannot cancel it through your wallet or reverse it on the chain. EIP-155 uses a chain ID when signing transactions to distinguish one chain’s transaction from another, as described in EIP-155. That helps prevent replay across chains; it does not move a confirmed transfer to the network you intended.

Recovery instead means accessing the received tokens with the key that controls the destination, asking the exchange that controls it to help, or using a contract’s available recovery mechanism. If the funds went to a contract with no method to release them, there may be no recovery path.

How do I avoid choosing the wrong network next time?

Before confirming, compare the sending network, receiving network, token, and destination address with the recipient’s instructions. A matching-looking address alone is not enough: exchanges may accept deposits only through specific networks, and an EVM token on Polygon is not automatically the same deposit as that token on Arbitrum. For a bridge transfer, check the route and the final destination chain shown before signing.

For a large transfer, send a small test amount first when fees and minimum deposit rules make that practical. Wait until it arrives at the expected destination, then send the rest. Keep the transaction hash and destination details so you can quickly establish what happened if something goes wrong.

  • Find the transaction hash on the chain where it was confirmed.
  • For your own EVM wallet, add that network and fund gas before moving tokens.
  • For an exchange address, contact support with the hash and ask about its recovery process.
  • A confirmed transfer cannot be cancelled; recovery depends on who controls the receiving address.
