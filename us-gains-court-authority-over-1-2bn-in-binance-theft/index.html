US gains court authority over $1.2bn in Binance theft

The Justice Department has court authority to seize or restrain $1.2 billion of Binance hack proceeds, while more than $2.7 billion is frozen overall.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 15, 2026 · 11:14 UTC3 min read

The Justice Department says it has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, putting part of a wider freeze into a formal legal recovery process. The department’s Nov. 12 announcement says combined U.S. government and private-sector efforts have frozen more than $2.7 billion since the theft.

The distinction matters: the $1.2 billion is not described as money already returned to Binance or its users. It is covered by court authorization for seizure or restraint; the wider $2.7 billion figure refers to frozen assets. The Justice Department says efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the stolen funds are ongoing, but does not say how much of the $1.2 billion is already under U.S. control. The Associated Press reports that a forfeiture claim would typically go before a judge, who would determine who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds.

The FBI says the theft occurred on or about Oct. 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets. The Justice Department identifies the perpetrators as members of the North Korean military, referred to by the FBI as “TraderTraitor.” The department says the group continues to launder assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. That gives the recovery effort two distinct tasks: tracing the funds as they move, then obtaining legal authority and cooperation from services that may be able to freeze or restrain them.

At about $6.3 billion, the Binance theft is described by AP as the largest crypto heist in history. The more than $2.7 billion frozen is a substantial intervention, but remains less than half of the reported loss; the court-authorized $1.2 billion is a subset of that frozen amount. These figures mark different stages—reported theft, freezes and legal authority—rather than a single recovered total. A freeze also does not establish that victims have been repaid: ownership and forfeiture remain separate questions.

The approach depends on cooperation beyond U.S. agencies. The Justice Department credits virtual asset service providers with helping interdict funds. Its account says the stolen assets continue to move through bridges, mixers and exchanges, but does not specify how much of the freeze came from any one company or method. Services that can restrain assets they control may help interrupt that movement; where the funds go next, and whether a service can act on them, affects how much can be recovered.

The same Nov. 12 release covered a separate North Korean social-engineering operation called FaceStealer, which officials say stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over 18 months. That figure is not part of the Binance recovery total: the release describes a different operation and enforcement action. Keeping the two cases distinct also clarifies what the $1.2 billion authorization measures.

The next signals are whether prosecutors file forfeiture claims, how courts resolve ownership, and whether service providers report further freezes as the assets move. Until those steps play out, the $1.2 billion marks legal reach over part of the stolen funds, not a completed recovery.
