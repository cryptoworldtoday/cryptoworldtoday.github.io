US court clears seizure of $1.2bn in Binance hack funds

US authorities secured court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of Binance hack proceeds tied to North Korea, while tracing and freezes continue.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 13, 2026 · 20:39 UTC3 min read

US authorities have court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step toward recovering part of a breach the FBI attributes to North Korean hackers. The Associated Press report says the Justice Department obtained the authorization on Thursday, Nov. 12.

The legal step covers more than $1.2 billion of the over $2.7 billion that U.S. government agencies and private-sector partners say they have frozen since the theft. The amount stolen was about $6.3 billion, according to AP. So the authorized sum is less than half of what has been frozen and about one fifth of the estimated theft. Those figures describe different stages: a freeze blocks movement, while seizure and restraint are actions authorized by a court.

The Justice Department’s announcement says the theft occurred on or about Oct. 29 and identifies the North Korean military actors as “TraderTraitor,” the FBI’s name for the group. The department says investigators and law-enforcement partners worked with virtual asset service providers to interdict the funds. Its account does not say that the full $1.2 billion has been returned to Binance or its customers.

That distinction matters because cryptocurrency transfers can move through multiple services and networks. The Justice Department says the actors continue to launder the stolen assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges, and that efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit them are ongoing. The same announcement describes cooperation with service providers as part of the effort. That mechanism can stop assets held or processed by cooperating services, but tracing and legal claims remain necessary as funds move across different venues.

The seizure authorization also does not settle who ultimately owns the restrained assets. AP notes that the government typically files a forfeiture claim in court, where a judge determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. That leaves a gap between preventing some assets from moving and establishing where they should go. The department’s announcement says it has court authorization to seize or restrain the funds; it does not describe a final forfeiture judgment or a distribution to affected users.

For scale, the reported theft exceeds the $1.5 billion Bybit theft in 2025 that AP described as the previous largest crypto heist. The current case therefore sets a new benchmark for reported losses, while the recovery figure remains a fraction of the total. The comparison also shows why a large freeze is not the same as recovery: more than $2.7 billion has been frozen, but the court-authorized amount is smaller, and the rest of the stolen assets remain subject to tracing and restraint efforts.

The next signals are whether authorities obtain forfeiture judgments, how much of the remaining frozen balance becomes subject to court action, and whether investigators and service providers can intercept funds as they pass through bridges, mixers and exchanges. Until those steps are reported, the confirmed milestone is legal authority over more than $1.2 billion, not a completed return of that amount.
