Citi takes tokenized deposits to Japan and UAE

Citi has added Japan and the UAE to its blockchain-based deposit service, extending 24/7 transfers across seven markets while testing whether private bank rails can connect.

By The Coin Wire EditorialOct 22, 2026 · 15:21 UTC3 min read

Citi has expanded its tokenized-deposit service into Japan and the United Arab Emirates, bringing its private blockchain network to seven markets and extending round-the-clock transfers for corporate and financial-institution clients, the bank said in its September 28 announcement.

The move adds two financial centers to a service already operating in the United States, Ireland, Hong Kong, Singapore and the United Kingdom. Citi says clients in Japan can move U.S. dollars through the service, while clients in the UAE can use U.S. dollars and euros. Transfers can move between a client’s accounts or to other clients in locations already enabled, without banking cut-off times or holiday calendars.

What does Citi’s expansion let clients do?

The service uses tokenized deposits and a private, permissioned blockchain to move money within Citi’s network. Citi says it supports near-instant payments, liquidity management and collateral management, and is already processing billions of dollars in transactions. The bank describes the tokens as a way to move commercial bank money; this is not a launch of a public cryptocurrency or a claim that deposits have left the banking system.

That distinction shapes the offer. A business can move liquidity outside standard banking hours while staying within Citi’s infrastructure and client relationships. The expansion does not establish that any customer can send funds to any wallet or bank at any time: transfers depend on the participating accounts, enabled locations and currencies. Nor does the announcement give transaction volumes by market or say how many clients are using the service.

How does Citi’s network compare with Swift’s?

Citi is expanding its own network, while Swift is building a shared layer to connect tokenized deposits issued on participating banks’ separate ledgers. Swift said in July that 17 banks across six continents were preparing to pilot live transactions on its blockchain-based ledger. The Swift announcement says the ledger can coordinate 24/7 movement of bank-issued deposits before final settlement through existing systems.

The difference is one of reach and maturity. Citi’s service is live in seven markets and operates across Citi’s network; Swift’s initial use was described as a controlled phase with banks preparing to pilot transactions across institutions. Citi also says it is investing in connectivity with emerging multi-bank tokenized networks, suggesting that a bank-specific service and shared infrastructure could eventually work together. The announcements do not establish that Citi’s seven-market service and Swift’s ledger are already interoperable.

There is also a speed comparison with existing payment rails. Swift said 75% of payments on its network reach beneficiary banks within 10 minutes, often in seconds. That means the case for blockchain is not simply that every payment on current systems is slow. The added proposition is movement beyond cut-off windows, with programmable transfers and liquidity across time zones. Whether those features outweigh the cost and coordination of new systems depends on how clients use them.

What will show whether the shift is spreading?

The next signals are practical: whether Citi adds more markets and currencies, whether more corporate clients use the service, and whether banks connect their separate systems. Swift’s pilots will show whether a shared ledger can move tokenized deposits across institutions while completing settlement on existing rails. For now, the shift is clearest in institutional payments and treasury operations. Broader access, interoperability and routine use remain questions to watch.
