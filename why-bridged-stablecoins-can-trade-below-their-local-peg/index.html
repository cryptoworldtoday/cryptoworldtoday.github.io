Why bridged stablecoins can trade below their local peg

Bridged stablecoins can slip below a dollar when redemption routes, liquidity or trust differ from the issuer-backed token, even if both share a ticker.

By The Coin Wire EditorialOct 17, 2026 · 03:42 UTC6 min read

A bridged stablecoin can trade below a dollar on one chain because buyers are pricing the route back to usable dollars, not just the token’s stated peg. The discount reflects the cost and uncertainty of converting that particular onchain claim into another asset or chain. That is different from a broad loss of confidence in the issuer: two tokens called USDC can have different prices if one is an issuer-backed token and the other is a bridge-created representation.

Before cross-chain tokens became common, traders mostly compared a stablecoin’s market price with its redemption value and the depth of its local trading pools. Bridging adds another layer: who created the destination token, what backs it, and how quickly it can be exchanged or redeemed. A transfer may involve locking tokens on one chain and minting a representation on another, or using a pool or relayer to deliver funds before settlement. Each route has its own liquidity and operational dependencies. A Biswap guide to moving treasury funds across chains gives more detail on the transfer mechanics; the key pricing point is that a bridge route can add costs and dependencies beyond the stablecoin itself.

What makes a bridged stablecoin different from the original?

A bridged stablecoin is a token representation whose link to the original depends on a bridge mechanism and a path for conversion. In a lock-and-mint design, tokens on the source chain are locked while corresponding tokens are issued on the destination chain. In a liquidity-based route, a service may pay the recipient from funds already available on the destination chain, then settle or rebalance later. The recipient gets speed, but the route relies on liquidity and settlement working as expected.

By comparison, some issuers support native tokens on several chains and operate a burn-and-mint transfer system between them. That can avoid a third-party wrapped representation, though it still involves the issuer’s supported chains, transfer process and fees. A native token’s availability on a chain does not mean every token with the same ticker there is the same asset. Contract address and issuer documentation matter more than the label shown by a wallet or exchange.

Why does the local price fall below a dollar?

The local market price is set by the orders available on that chain and venue. If a bridged token has fewer buyers, a sale large enough to move the pool can push its price below the issuer-backed version. Arbitrageurs may buy the cheaper token and convert it through a bridge or swap route, but they will do so only when the expected gain exceeds fees, delay, capital costs and the chance that conversion fails or takes longer than expected.

Several forces can widen the gap:

  • Thin liquidity: A small pool means a modest trade can move the quoted price, and a large seller may accept a bigger discount.
  • Conversion costs: Swap fees, bridge fees, gas and price impact reduce the value of arbitrage.
  • Time and settlement: A route that takes longer ties up capital and exposes arbitrageurs to price changes before they can sell.
  • Backing and trust: Traders may demand a discount if they are unsure whether the bridge can release or settle the underlying tokens.

These forces interact. A discount can appear even when the bridge is operating normally: thin destination liquidity plus a surge in selling may be enough. Conversely, a deep pool may keep the displayed price close to a dollar while the route remains dependent on a bridge whose security or settlement model traders do not trust. The screen price alone does not reveal which situation applies.

How should a reader compare the available routes?

Compare the token and the exit route together. A native issuer token is often the simpler choice for someone who needs issuer redemption, provided the issuer supports that chain and the holder can use the required account or service. A bridged token can be more convenient for onchain activity where it has deep liquidity, but its market price may reflect the bridge’s own conversion conditions.

For a small transfer, a lower-fee bridge can be worthwhile even if it delivers a wrapped representation. For a larger treasury balance, the relevant comparison is the full cost and reliability of moving back to the chain where funds will be spent or redeemed. Check the exact token contract, bridge route, quoted output, destination liquidity and whether the intended receiving venue accepts that specific token. Do not assume that a ticker match guarantees interchangeability.

There is also a trade-off between speed and settlement design. Relayers can front destination liquidity and deliver quickly, while later settlement repays them. A route that waits for a more direct cross-chain process may reduce reliance on fronted liquidity but take longer. Neither structure removes cost; it allocates capital use, delay and trust differently. The best option depends on whether the recipient values immediate availability, issuer redemption, or minimizing conversion slippage.

What signals show whether the discount is temporary?

Look for evidence that arbitrage can close the gap. A recovering price alongside deeper pool liquidity and tighter conversion quotes suggests that buyers can move tokens between markets at reasonable cost. A persistent discount despite apparent price differences may indicate that the route is expensive, constrained, or viewed as risky.

Useful signals include the spread between the bridged token and the issuer-backed version on the same chain, pool depth near the current price, bridge availability and settlement status, and the actual amount returned by a test-sized conversion. Also check whether the issuer lists the token contract as supported for redemption; a familiar ticker is not proof. If a bridge pauses transfers or destination liquidity drains, the local discount can widen even while the underlying issuer-backed stablecoin remains near its peg.

The practical takeaway is to treat a bridged stablecoin as a specific chain-and-contract asset, not as a dollar interchangeable everywhere. Its price should converge toward a dollar when credible conversion paths remain open and arbitrage is profitable. The next clues are whether those paths stay available, liquidity returns, and the discount narrows after conversion costs.
