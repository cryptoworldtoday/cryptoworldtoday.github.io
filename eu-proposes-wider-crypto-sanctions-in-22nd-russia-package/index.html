EU proposes wider crypto sanctions in 22nd Russia package

The Commission’s 22nd Russia sanctions proposal would extend bank and crypto restrictions, but member-state approval is still needed before measures take effect.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 24, 2026 · 13:02 UTC3 min read

The European Commission has proposed extending Russia sanctions to more banks and crypto platforms, including networks in third countries accused of helping Russia evade restrictions. In its 22nd sanctions package statement on 23 November, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the proposal would extend transaction bans to about 60 Russian banks, remove all 60 from SWIFT and establish a Crypto Sanctions Task Force. The measures are proposed, not yet in force: the Associated Press report says all 27 EU member states must endorse them, a process that can take weeks and may change the package.

What would change for crypto platforms?

The Commission’s statement says the proposed crackdown would focus on Russia’s crypto and payment networks while also including third countries that help Russia evade sanctions. It does not name platforms or specify the proposed restrictions’ legal mechanics. Von der Leyen said the new task force would support that work and that the EU wanted to align efforts with international partners.

The focus on crypto builds on measures already adopted. In July, the Council of the EU said its 21st package extended transaction bans to 14 crypto-related service platforms based in Georgia, Panama, the United Arab Emirates, the Marshall Islands, Kyrgyzstan and Belarus. It also introduced the possibility of a full third-country ban on crypto-asset services, allowing the EU to prohibit transactions between EU operators and crypto providers used by Russia. That package was adopted; the 22nd package remains a Commission proposal.

How does the proposal compare with earlier sanctions?

The proposed bank measures are wider in stated number than the July package’s specific banking steps: the Council then reported transaction bans for 33 additional Russian financial institutions, alongside asset freezes affecting 94 banks and major financial institutions. The new proposal would extend transaction bans to about 60 Russian banks and remove those banks from SWIFT, according to von der Leyen. The figures describe different measures and should not be read as a direct count of newly sanctioned institutions.

Crypto enforcement is part of a broader package, not a standalone digital-asset policy. The Commission also proposed listing more than 70 shadow-fleet ships, banning Russian-insured tankers from EU ports, expanding energy-service restrictions and barring Russian teams and athletes from competing under the Russian flag at EU sporting events. The stated aim is to restrict channels used to finance Russia’s war and to counter sanctions evasion. The reach into third countries could widen the pressure on intermediaries, while making the rules’ eventual scope dependent on member-state agreement and the final legal text.

What should crypto firms watch next?

The next signal is whether the 27 member states approve the package and what changes emerge during negotiations. For crypto businesses, the key details to watch are which platforms or networks are named, how the third-country measures are defined, and when any transaction restrictions would apply. Until those terms are published and adopted, the announcement sets out the Commission’s intended direction rather than new obligations for EU operators.
