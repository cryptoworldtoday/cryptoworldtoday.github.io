SyncSwap Slippage Starts With the Minimum You’ll Accept

Slippage tolerance sets the output floor for a swap, not a guaranteed price; choose it by checking minimum received against volatility, route depth and trade size.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 3, 2026 · 17:16 UTC5 min read

SyncSwap slippage tolerance sets the least output a swap can deliver before the transaction reverts. That makes the setting a trade-off: a tighter floor protects the amount you receive but can reject a quote that shifts before execution; a looser floor gives the route more room to complete, while allowing a worse result. The useful setting is the one whose minimum received still makes the trade acceptable.

A swap quote is an estimate based on available pools and the size of your trade. As other trades change pool prices, the output can move between the quote and execution. SyncSwap’s router can split a swap across paths and checks the combined output against a minimum; if the amount falls short, the swap reverts. For more on why tokens may take several routes, read why SyncSwap uses three token paths. The route can improve the quote, but it cannot make that quote fixed.

What does slippage tolerance change?

Slippage tolerance changes the minimum output the transaction will accept. If the quoted output is 100 units and the tolerance is 1%, the minimum is roughly 99 units. A successful trade can return anything from that floor up to the quoted amount or better; the setting does not promise the estimate.

This floor is separate from price impact. Price impact is the effect the trade itself has on the pool price as it uses liquidity. Slippage tolerance is how much the output may move from the quote before the transaction is rejected. A large trade against a shallow pool can have substantial price impact before it is submitted. Raising tolerance does not reduce that cost; it only permits execution at a lower output.

There is a similar distinction between a swap’s quoted rate and its minimum received amount. The quote helps compare routes and decide whether the trade is worthwhile. Minimum received is the execution condition. Look at both: a favorable quote paired with a low floor can leave you accepting a result you would have rejected if you had checked the final limit.

How should you choose a minimum you can live with?

Start with the output, then work backward to the tolerance. Ask how many destination tokens you need for the trade to remain worthwhile. Compare that amount with the minimum received shown in the swap details. If the displayed floor falls below your acceptable amount, tighten the tolerance or reconsider the trade size and timing.

Pool depth, volatility, trade size and route length all affect how likely a quote is to move before execution. A liquid pair that is trading steadily may need less room than a volatile token with scattered liquidity. A routed swap may also rely on several pool steps: each can contribute to changes in the final output. A single percentage cannot fit every token pair or trade.

  • For a small trade in a liquid pair, begin with a relatively tight tolerance and check that the minimum remains acceptable.
  • For a volatile pair or a longer route, expect more movement, but decide the lowest acceptable output before allowing more room.
  • For a large trade, compare the quote and minimum received with the trade’s size; consider whether reducing the amount would produce a more acceptable result.
  • If a transaction fails, refresh the quote and inspect the route before changing the setting. A wider tolerance is a choice to accept a lower floor, not a repair for poor liquidity.

These are decision rules, not universal percentages. The amount shown on the screen matters more than a setting that looks familiar from another pair. If the minimum received is hard to judge, pause and check the token and amount before approving the swap.

When is a failed swap better than a wider tolerance?

A failed swap is preferable when the alternative is accepting output below your own floor. If the market moves beyond the tolerance before execution, the router’s minimum-output check causes the swap to revert rather than complete at that lower amount. You may still incur a network fee for a failed transaction, depending on the network and transaction, so repeated retries are not costless.

When a swap fails, first refresh the quote. Check whether the expected output changed, whether the route changed, and whether the minimum still meets your needs. If the new estimate itself is poor, wait or reduce the trade size instead of simply widening the tolerance. If the estimate remains reasonable but the output is moving quickly, you can choose a lower floor—but make that decision from the amount you would accept, not just from a desire to make the transaction go through.

The practical test is straightforward: before confirming, read the estimated output and minimum received as two different figures. The first describes the current route; the second describes the boundary you are agreeing to. Watch whether the quote and route remain stable when refreshed, whether a smaller amount changes the price impact, and whether the minimum still clears your own threshold. Those signals tell you more than the tolerance percentage on its own.
