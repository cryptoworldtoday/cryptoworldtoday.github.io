DEX Interfaces and On-Chain Trade Settlement

What a DEX interface does versus the protocol behind it: the app shapes your order and quote; smart contracts enforce the rules and settle token transfers.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 21, 2026 · 03:37 UTC4 min read

A DEX interface prepares a trade for your wallet, while the protocol’s smart contracts enforce the trade rules and settle the token transfers. The interface shows choices and builds the request you sign; the on-chain contracts determine whether that signed request can execute.

A trade starts in the interface

The interface turns your choices into a proposed order. Say you want to trade one token for another: the page lets you select the tokens and amount, shows an estimated quote, and builds a signing request for your wallet. You review and sign that request; the app does not itself move tokens between wallets.

On cowswap, that request is an intent: a signed message stating what you want to trade and the conditions you will accept. Solvers compete to find a way to fill it, then submit a settlement through CoW Protocol’s contract. When comparing routes on cowswap, review the quote and order details before signing; cowswap solvers determine how an intent is filled within the limits you signed.

The quote is an estimate for the route and conditions shown by the app. Prices can move before settlement, and the interface may display network costs or other fees alongside the expected amount. A signed intent sets constraints for execution; it is not the same as a completed trade.

The protocol applies the rules and settles

The protocol’s contracts check the signed request and handle settlement on-chain. With CoW Protocol, the settlement contract verifies the user’s signature and the trade conditions before the tokens move. Solvers propose settlements, but the contract applies the rules that determine whether those proposals are valid.

This separates choosing what you want from working out how to execute it. CoW Protocol groups intents into batch auctions, where solvers compete to find valid ways to settle them. A solver may match compatible orders or source liquidity elsewhere. The user-facing interface helps form and review the order; the contract governs settlement.

Uniswap offers a useful comparison. The Uniswap app is a front end to the Uniswap Protocol contracts: the app gives users a way to choose a swap and submit it, while the contracts execute the protocol’s on-chain rules. In both cases, a familiar website is one access point to a system whose settlement logic lives in smart contracts on Ethereum.

That distinction helps explain why two apps using the same protocol can show different amounts. An app can add its own fee on top of protocol costs, and apps can differ in the routes or token information they present. The amount shown to you is therefore shaped by both the protocol and the particular interface. Compare the expected amount after fees, the network cost, and the order’s execution limits before signing; a familiar token name or a headline quote alone does not show the full cost.

The website is an access point, not the settlement layer

If a DEX website goes down, its contracts and their on-chain state remain on the network, and tokens that have not been transferred remain in your wallet. But you may lose access to that particular screen or its quote. Another interface may offer access to the same protocol, though its features, token display, and fees can differ.

The boundary matters for trust. A front end can make trading easier, but it cannot replace the checks enforced by the contracts. Before you sign, confirm the token, amount, recipient, and conditions in your wallet. The interface prepares the request; your wallet approves it; the protocol’s contracts settle a valid trade.

Questions traders ask

Can I trade on CoW Protocol without using cowswap?

Yes. CoW Swap is an interface built on CoW Protocol, and other wallets or apps can integrate with the protocol. Each interface may present different tools, quotes, token information, and fees, so check the order details wherever you trade.

Who controls the interface’s token list?

The interface determines which tokens it displays and how it labels them. A token appearing in an app is not, by itself, proof that it is the token you intend to trade. Compare its contract address with a trusted source before signing.

Can a fake DEX website take my funds?

A fake page can trick you into approving token access or signing an order with unwanted terms, such as a different recipient. Review what the wallet asks you to approve or sign, and verify the website address and transaction details before confirming.

Do interface fees go to the protocol?

Not necessarily. An interface fee may go to the app or its operator, while protocol fees and network costs are separate charges. Check the fee breakdown in the trade screen and wallet before you sign.
