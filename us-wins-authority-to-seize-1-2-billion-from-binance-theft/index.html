US wins authority to seize $1.2 billion from Binance theft

The Justice Department won court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion from Binance’s October theft, while broader freezes and tracing continue.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 15, 2026 · 03:33 UTC3 min read

U.S. authorities have obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step that moves part of the recovery effort beyond private-sector freezes and into federal forfeiture proceedings. The Justice Department said on Nov. 12 that the theft occurred on or about Oct. 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets, according to its announcement of the seizure and related enforcement actions.

The amount covered by the court authorization is part of a larger pool: U.S. agencies and private-sector partners have frozen more than $2.7 billion since the theft, the department said. The FBI attributes the attack to TraderTraitor, a North Korean military-linked group. The Associated Press reported that the FBI described the cryptocurrency as seized, while the Justice Department’s wording distinguishes the court-authorized seizure or restraint from the broader freeze total.

That distinction matters because a freeze can prevent assets from moving without deciding who ultimately owns them. A court-authorized seizure gives the government a legal route to take control or restrain specified assets, but it does not itself establish that the funds have been returned to Binance or to other claimants. The AP notes that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then determines whether anyone has a legitimate claim to recovered property.

The comparison with the overall theft shows both the scale and the limit of the action. The authorized amount is roughly one-fifth of the more than $6 billion reported stolen; the $2.7 billion frozen by government and industry partners is a larger share, but remains below half. Those figures describe different stages of intervention, not a final accounting of losses or recovery. The Justice Department did not say the remaining assets had been recovered.

Coordination with virtual asset service providers helped investigators freeze funds before they moved beyond law enforcement’s reach, according to officials quoted in the department’s release. That route can interrupt transfers across platforms, while a court order can support control of identified assets. Neither measure stops all laundering: investigators said the attackers continue to move funds through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. The FBI has urged service providers to block transactions involving addresses it linked to the theft.

For Binance and its customers, the announcement therefore marks a sizable intervention, not a resolution. The scale of the theft makes the $1.2 billion authorization significant, but the difference between assets frozen, assets subject to seizure or restraint, and assets ultimately forfeited remains central. The public statements do not specify which tokens or addresses are covered by the authorization, or when a forfeiture claim might be filed.

The next signals are whether investigators and cooperating platforms can identify and immobilize more of the stolen assets, whether the government files forfeiture claims, and what a court decides about competing claims to funds. The Justice Department says tracing, freezing, seizing and forfeiting the assets are ongoing. Until those steps produce a final disposition, the announced figure measures legal authority over part of the stolen crypto, not money returned.
