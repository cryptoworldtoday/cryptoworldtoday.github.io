Impermanent Loss Recovery at the Entry Price Ratio

Does impermanent loss go away if price comes back? Yes: in a 50/50 constant-product pool, it returns to zero when the token-price ratio returns to entry.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 20, 2026 · 16:24 UTC5 min read

Impermanent loss returns to zero when a 50/50 constant-product pool’s token-price ratio returns to its entry ratio, as long as you remain in the pool. The position’s value relative to simply holding the tokens depends on the current ratio, not the path prices took to get there.

That answer can sound at odds with the word “loss.” A price moves, your pool balance shifts, and the value gap against holding the tokens can remain visible even after a market rebound. The distinction that settles the question is whether the ratio between the two token prices has recovered—not whether either token has returned to its old dollar price.

What does “the price comes back” need to mean?

For impermanent loss to disappear, the price ratio must return to where it was when you deposited. A return in one token’s dollar price alone is not enough if the other token’s price has moved differently.

In a constant-product pool, the reserves follow x*y=k. Uniswap V2’s design uses this relationship to set prices as the pool’s token balances change. For a 50/50 position, arbitrage trades adjust those balances as the market ratio moves. You end up holding less of the token that rose relative to its pair and more of the one that fell.

That shift creates impermanent loss compared with keeping the original tokens outside the pool. The standard estimate for a price-ratio change r is 2√r/(1+r) − 1. A ratio change to 2 gives an approximate loss of 5.7%; a change to 4 gives about 20%. These figures compare the pool position with holding the original token amounts, before fees.

If the ratio later returns to entry, the pool’s balance mix returns to the mix expected at that ratio. The relative-value gap falls to zero. The pool does not remember whether the ratio rose first or fell first; the current ratio determines the comparison.

Why can a rebound still leave a loss?

A rebound leaves impermanent loss when the token-price ratio remains away from its entry point. Say you deposited two tokens when they were worth the same amount. One doubles while the other stays flat, then the rising token falls partway back. Its dollar price has retreated, but if it is still worth more relative to its pair than at deposit, the pool position still trails holding the original tokens.

The price may also never return to the entry ratio. Waiting keeps the position exposed to a possible recovery, but it does not guarantee one. Withdrawing while the ratio is off converts the current difference into your realized outcome: you receive the pool’s current token mix, rather than the original amounts. That decision also ends your exposure to later ratio moves and stops further fee earning.

Fees earned while you were providing liquidity remain yours when you withdraw. They can offset some or all of the impermanent loss, but they do not change its calculation. Keep the two figures separate: impermanent loss compares the pool position with holding the tokens, while fees are additional income from providing liquidity.

Can impermanent loss be zero while you lose money?

Yes. Impermanent loss measures the relative performance of your pool position against holding the same tokens; it does not measure whether your dollar value has risen or fallen.

Take a pair in which both tokens fall 50% against dollars, at the same rate. Their price ratio stays unchanged, so the position has zero impermanent loss relative to holding those tokens. But the pool position’s dollar value has still roughly halved. A zero IL reading is not the same as a break-even investment.

This is why the ratio, rather than a chart of either token on its own, is the useful signal when deciding whether to wait or withdraw. Compare the current pair ratio with the ratio at your deposit. Then assess the position’s dollar value and fees separately. A pool can recover its relative performance and still be worth less in dollars than when you entered.

What should a returning LP watch?

A returning liquidity provider should track the entry ratio, the current ratio, the token amounts available on withdrawal, and fees earned. Together, those figures show whether the relative loss has recovered and what the position is worth now.

The practical test is simple: when the ratio returns to entry, impermanent loss returns to zero if you are still in the pool. Until then, waiting is a choice to keep the position exposed to the pair’s next move, while withdrawing accepts the current token mix and any loss against holding.
