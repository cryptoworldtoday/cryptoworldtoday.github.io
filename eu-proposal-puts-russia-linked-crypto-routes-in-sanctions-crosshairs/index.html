EU proposal puts Russia-linked crypto routes in sanctions crosshairs

The Commission’s 22nd Russia package would target crypto and payment networks, including evasion routes through third countries; all 27 EU states must approve it.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 01:01 UTC3 min read

The European Commission proposed a new Russia sanctions package on Nov. 23 that would target crypto and payment networks, including routes through third countries, adding a crypto sanctions task force to the EU’s enforcement effort. The Associated Press report on the proposal says the measures still need approval from all 27 member states and could change before taking effect.

The package is the EU’s 22nd round of sanctions, according to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. Its financial measures pair a proposed crackdown on crypto and payment networks with transaction restrictions on about 60 Russian banks, which the Commission says would also be removed from SWIFT, the international payments messaging system. The Commission president’s statement identifies third countries helping Russia evade sanctions as part of the crypto focus.

What would change for crypto services?

The proposal broadens the stated enforcement focus from Russian institutions to networks and intermediaries that could help Russia evade existing restrictions. The Commission has not, in its statement, named specific platforms or described the exact transactions, assets or service types that would be covered. It says the package will target crypto and payment networks in Russia and in third countries that help Russia evade sanctions.

That leaves a key distinction between the announced direction and an enforceable rule. The proposal signals broader scrutiny of crypto-related channels and cross-border payment links, but the statement does not provide a list of entities or a detailed test for when a third-country service would be caught. The proposed Crypto Sanctions Task Force is meant to support the work; its remit and operating details were not specified in the statement.

How does this compare with the existing pressure?

The Commission says the EU has already sanctioned more than 4,700 people and entities. The AP reports that this proposal comes just over a month after a large listing targeting almost 1,600 Russian officials and companies linked to the arms industry. The new package adds a different kind of pressure: financial access through banks and crypto or payment routes, alongside energy, travel and sporting measures.

The bank plan is more concrete in the announcement than the crypto plan: the Commission specifies a transaction ban for about 60 Russian banks and removal from SWIFT. For crypto, it describes the networks and evasion routes it wants to pursue, but leaves the operational scope open. That gap matters to platforms and intermediaries because the practical effect depends on which services are named and how the rules define assistance to sanctions evasion.

What still has to happen?

The proposal is not yet in force. The AP says all 27 EU countries must endorse it, a process that can take weeks, and that the measures may be modified. The Commission’s statement also describes other proposed steps, including listing more than 70 shadow-fleet vessels, restricting Russian ex-combatants’ visas, and barring Russian teams and athletes from competing under the Russian flag at EU sports events.

For crypto firms, the next signals are the final legal text, any named platforms or payment networks, and whether the third-country provisions specify a clear threshold for sanctions. Member-state approval will show whether the Commission’s proposal survives intact; implementation details will show how far the intended crackdown reaches beyond Russian-based services.
