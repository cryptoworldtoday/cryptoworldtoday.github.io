FinCEN orders U.S. institutions to block MEXC transfers

FinCEN ordered U.S. institutions to block transfers involving MEXC, citing $16.7 billion in alleged illicit flows; the order takes effect after Federal Register publication.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 9, 2026 · 22:11 UTC3 min read

FinCEN has ordered U.S. financial institutions to stop processing funds transfers involving MEXC Global, a move that could cut the crypto exchange off from U.S. banking channels once the order takes effect. The agency said its November 6 order responds to at least $16 billion in transactions it links to sanctions evasion and other illicit activity; its announcement of the order says the restriction covers transfers involving MEXC. The order is not yet in force: FinCEN’s document says it takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register, where it was still pending as of the document’s release.

What does the order block, and when?

It bars covered U.S. financial institutions from transmitting funds that involve MEXC Global, including its non-U.S. subsidiaries, branches, offices and successor entities. FinCEN is using Section 9714 of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act, which allows Treasury to prohibit certain fund transfers involving foreign financial institutions found to be of primary money-laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance.

That distinction matters because the action was announced under Operation Economic Outcast, Treasury’s campaign against Iran and its financial networks, while the formal finding supporting this order centers on Russian illicit finance. FinCEN says MEXC facilitated transactions benefiting Iran, North Korean financial facilitators and cybercrime actors as well. The order itself focuses on the Russian connection, rather than imposing a general ban on crypto trading or declaring MEXC’s entire global service closed.

How large is MEXC compared with earlier crypto targets?

FinCEN’s order says MEXC processed more than $1 billion in daily transactions and held about $5 billion in reserve assets as of June 2026. It puts the exchange among the world’s 20 largest by trading volume; at the end of 2025, the order says, MEXC had about 9% of the centralized exchange market, compared with 47.4% for the two largest platforms combined. The scale makes this a broader market test of the transfer restriction than earlier crypto cases.

CoinDesk reported that Bitzlato in 2023 and PM2BTC in 2024 were previous crypto targets under the provision, both much smaller than MEXC. FinCEN’s order alleges that between May 2021 and May 2026, MEXC facilitated $16.7 billion connected to Russian illicit finance and other illicit activity, including sanctions evasion and cybercrime. It also identifies nearly $1.5 billion in direct and indirect exposure to Russian sanctions-evasion transactions, and $232 million in direct transactions with entities sanctioned by the U.S., European Union or United Kingdom.

Among the examples cited are transfers involving sanctioned exchanges Garantex and Grinex. FinCEN says MEXC transacted directly with them after their designations, including as recently as June 2026. Those figures and examples are allegations and findings in the agency’s order, not independently established judicial conclusions.

What changes for users and what should be watched?

The restriction targets U.S. financial institutions, not every route by which MEXC users around the world can trade or transfer crypto. It does, however, constrain a key set of fiat and crypto transfer connections: U.S. institutions will be prohibited from processing covered transfers once the order is effective. That could affect legitimate as well as suspect transactions involving the exchange, while leaving the scope of the practical disruption dependent on how many counterparties and payment routes rely on U.S. institutions.

CoinDesk reported that MEXC said it was reviewing the order, continuing to operate normally and commissioning an independent review of its anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls. The next signals are the order’s Federal Register publication date, which starts the 30-day clock, and any change to MEXC’s operations or to the access its users have through U.S.-linked financial institutions.
