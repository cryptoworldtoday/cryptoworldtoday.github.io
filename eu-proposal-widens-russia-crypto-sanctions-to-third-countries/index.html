EU proposal widens Russia crypto sanctions to third countries

The EU's proposed 22nd Russia package widens crypto enforcement to payment networks and third countries, but all 27 members must approve it before it takes effect.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 24, 2026 · 13:39 UTC3 min read

The European Commission has proposed extending its Russia sanctions to crypto and payment networks in third countries that help Moscow evade restrictions, adding a new enforcement focus to the bloc’s 22nd package. In her statement on the proposal on 23 November, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the measures would also target about 60 Russian banks and remove them from SWIFT. The crypto provisions’ targets and operational details were not specified.

The proposal would expand pressure beyond Russian institutions to services in countries that facilitate sanctions evasion. The Associated Press report says the measures need endorsement from all 27 EU member countries before taking effect; that process can take weeks, and the plans may change.

The proposal builds on an existing crypto sanctions tool

The new package follows the EU’s 21st sanctions package, adopted in July. The Commission said that round extended transaction bans to additional crypto platforms and created a mechanism for a full third-country ban on crypto-asset services when platforms facilitate sanctions circumvention. The Council said the mechanism could block transactions between EU operators and any crypto provider used by Russia.

That earlier step established a route for action against services hosted outside Russia. The 22nd proposal signals that the Commission wants to use a wider network-based approach: von der Leyen said the new focus would cover Russia and third countries that help it evade sanctions. She also said the EU had launched a Crypto Sanctions Task Force to support the work and align efforts with international partners. The announcement did not identify specific platforms, countries or transactions the proposed measures would cover.

What the banking and crypto measures would change

The banking proposal has a more specific headline number: about 60 Russian banks would face a transaction ban and be removed from SWIFT, the messaging system used for international payments. The crypto announcement gives no comparable count of platforms or providers. Instead, it describes a target category—crypto and payment networks used to circumvent sanctions—and a geographic reach that includes third countries.

That distinction matters for enforcement. A ban on a named provider can give firms a clear counterparty to screen, while action against a network or services in a third country may reach more routes for moving value. It also depends on identifying which services fall within scope and how the EU will connect them to sanctions evasion. The Commission has not yet published those details in von der Leyen’s statement.

The proposal also combines financial restrictions with measures beyond crypto: bans on Russian teams and athletes competing under the Russian flag at EU sporting events, restrictions on official symbols, and limits on Russian sponsorship of European sports and cultural associations. Those provisions would broaden the package’s reach, but do not change the crypto measures’ still-unspecified scope.

Approval and implementation are the next tests

The proposal is not yet an adopted sanctions regime. According to AP, all 27 member states must endorse it, and the terms may be modified during that process. Until then, the new crypto restrictions remain proposed measures, while the July package’s third-country crypto tool is already part of the adopted framework.

The signals to watch are whether member states agree to the package, how the final text defines crypto and payment networks, and whether it names providers or sets criteria for identifying third-country services that help Russia evade sanctions. The task force’s role and coordination with international partners will also matter: the proposal’s reach depends on how the EU turns its broad target into rules firms can apply.
