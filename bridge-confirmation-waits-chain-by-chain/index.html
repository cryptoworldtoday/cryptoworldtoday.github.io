Bridge confirmation waits, chain by chain

How many block confirmations do bridges wait for on each chain? Most follow finality: about 13 minutes on Ethereum, seconds on Solana, with bridge-specific thresholds.

By The Coin Wire EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 04:34 UTC4 min read

After a transfer, the wait depends on the bridge’s threshold for the source chain: most bridges release funds after the deposit reaches their chosen level of finality, because a reorg could otherwise undo it. There is no universal confirmation count for Ethereum, Solana, Arbitrum, Base or Polygon; the bridge’s own policy sets the number, and Circle publishes per-chain requirements for CCTP attestations.

How many block confirmations do bridges wait for on each chain?

Each bridge chooses its own threshold, so the useful comparison is the bridge’s table for your route and transfer type. For an ordinary USDC bridge transfer, the source chain’s finality rule usually matters more than a single count copied from another provider. A USDC bridge helps move USDC between blockchains; before using one, compare its stated wait with the chain’s finality model. A lower count can mean a faster release, while a longer wait gives the bridge more confidence the source transaction will hold.

How long do bridges wait on Ethereum, Arbitrum and Base?

Ethereum finalizes after two epochs of 32 slots, with 12 seconds per slot, or roughly 13 minutes under normal conditions. That is a protocol finality measure, not a fixed block-count rule every bridge must use. For instance, Circle’s CCTP Standard Transfer table uses about 65 Ethereum block confirmations and reports typical attestation availability in roughly 15 to 19 minutes.

Arbitrum and Base are Ethereum rollups, so their standard waits can depend on when a batch is posted to Ethereum and when its L1 block finalizes. Circle lists about 65 Ethereum blocks for Standard Transfer on both chains. In a constructed case, someone sending USDC from Arbitrum to another chain should read “65 ETH blocks” as a wait tied to Ethereum finality, not 65 Arbitrum blocks.

What does “finalized” mean on Solana?

On Solana, “finalized” marks a block the cluster recognizes as finalized after validator votes, rather than a universal number of confirmations. Bridges that wait for this commitment typically wait seconds, though elapsed time and confirmation count depend on the bridge’s design. Circle’s CCTP table, for example, lists 32 blocks for Standard Transfer and two to three blocks for Fast Transfer.

The distinction matters when a bridge accepts an earlier state such as “confirmed.” That may make funds available sooner, but it is a different threshold from waiting for the strongest finalized commitment. Check which commitment the bridge names before comparing its wait with another service’s stated count.

What is the difference between Fast Transfer and Standard Transfer in CCTP?

CCTP Standard Transfer waits for hard finality before Circle attests to a transfer; Fast Transfer can attest at soft finality, typically within seconds. The faster option accepts more reorganization risk, which Circle manages in part with a global allowance. Standard favors stronger source-chain assurance; Fast favors speed.

Circle’s per-chain table makes the difference concrete: Ethereum is listed at two blocks for Fast Transfer and about 65 for Standard; Arbitrum and Base are one block for Fast and about 65 Ethereum blocks for Standard. Those figures describe CCTP attestations, not every bridge using those chains. Treat the table as a policy reference for CCTP, then compare the bridge you are actually using.

Why does Polygon PoS use milestones instead of a simple count?

Polygon PoS finality is milestone-based, so a bridge may track the network’s finality milestone instead of waiting for a fixed number of ordinary blocks. Circle’s CCTP Standard Transfer table lists two to three Polygon confirmations, with an average attestation time of about eight seconds. Other bridges can choose different policies, so a raw count is meaningful only alongside the finality rule it represents.

Do intent bridges wait for source-chain finality before paying out?

Intent bridges such as Across can pay the destination before the source transaction reaches finality; settlement and verification happen internally afterward. That shifts the wait away from the user’s visible payout and onto the bridge’s settlement process. The trade-off is speed against reliance on the bridge’s liquidity and settlement design. For an occasional user who wants funds quickly, that can be useful; when the priority is waiting for the source transaction itself to finalize, a bridge that holds payout until finality is the clearer fit.

Why is my bridge stuck waiting for confirmations?

A bridge may still be waiting for its threshold, for an Ethereum batch to finalize, or for a finality milestone to be recognized. Compare the source transaction’s status with the bridge’s own per-chain policy and, for CCTP, Circle’s published table. A large number displayed by one service may count Ethereum blocks, while another may use the source chain’s finalized state; the label and chain matter as much as the count.
